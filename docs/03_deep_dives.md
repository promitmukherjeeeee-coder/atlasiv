## 1. Resources and production chains

### 1.1 Layers

Every material has a place in one chain, and each step is built on the one before it.

| Layer | What it is | Examples | Behavior |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L0 Natural | Found in nature | Quartz, iron ore, bauxite, coal, oil, uranium, timber, water, soil, animals | Deposits, discovery, depletion, extraction yield, mineral families |
| L1 Refined | Processed from L0 | Silicon, steel, aluminium, glass, plastics, fuels, enriched uranium | Needs energy, tech and infrastructure. Has purity levels |
| Grade 1 | First man-made components | Transistors, engines, bearings, batteries, optics | Recipes, scale effects, tradable |
| Grade 2 | Integrated assemblies | SoCs, turbines, machine tools, aircraft | Needs Grade 1 plus skilled labor |
| Grade 3 | Complex systems | Supercomputers, fabs, power plants, networks | Mostly built as infrastructure assets |
| Grade 4 | Frontier | Quantum computers, fusion reactors, advanced AI systems | Extreme inputs, very few sites worldwide |

The four man-made grades apply per chain, so every chain climbs at most four grades above refined materials. Grades are not universal power multipliers. They differ by recipe complexity, tolerance, purity, reliability and integration requirements, so a G3 power station and a G3 computing facility perform differently.

### 1.2 Resources, infrastructure and capabilities

- **Resources** are consumed stocks and flows.
- **Infrastructure assets** are built, sit in a district or region, have an owner, location, operating capacity, labor demand, maintenance, efficiency, condition, technical compatibility, residual lifetime and vulnerability. Wear, damage, maintenance backlog, technological obsolescence, economic stranding and destruction are tracked separately. Manufactured goods become capital through construction, installation and commissioning, and ownership is separate from operational control.
- **Capabilities** are what assets deliver: compute capacity, grid capacity, refining capacity, research speed.
- Example: a supercomputer consumes SoCs, energy, cooling and specialists. It delivers compute capacity that speeds research, weather forecasting, finance, surveillance and weapons design.

### 1.3 Rules

- **Recipe graph.** Each recipe has inputs, outputs, energy, labor skill, tech, byproducts and waste.
- **Unlock chain.** Discovering a deposit unlocks a research option. Research unlocks the recipe. An asset of the previous grade unlocks the next grade (bauxite, then smelting, then aluminium).
- **Co-products.** One deposit yields several materials (for example gallium from bauxite processing).
- **Purity and quality.** Higher grades need higher purity, which needs more energy and better process control.
- **Substitution.** Some inputs can be swapped at a cost in performance.
- **Bottleneck materials.** A few inputs gate whole chains (high-purity quartz, rare earths, helium for cooling), which creates monopolies and sanctions.
- **Learning curves.** Cost falls with accumulated production.
- **Site needs.** Power, water, clean air, skilled labor, transport. Few places qualify for high grades.
- **Trade in intermediates.** Chains span borders, so supply chains become strategic.
- **Recycling loops.** Scrap and end-of-life recovery feed back into earlier layers.
- **Obsolescence.** Newer grades make older ones cheap, and infrastructure built on them gets stranded.
- **Failure modes.** Contamination, accidents, sabotage, supply cutoffs.

### 1.4 Example chains

| Chain | Steps |
| --- | --- |
| Silicon and compute | Quartz, metallurgical silicon, polysilicon, wafers, transistors (G1), SoCs (G2), supercomputers (G3), quantum computers (G4) |
| Iron and steel | Ore and coal, pig iron, steel, alloys, machine tools (G2), industrial plants (G3) |
| Aluminium | Bauxite, alumina, aluminium (power hungry), alloys, airframes (G2) |
| Nuclear | Uranium ore, concentrate, enriched fuel, reactors (G3), breeder systems (G4) |
| Fossil and chemical | Oil, refined fractions, plastics and fertilizers, advanced polymers (G1), engineered materials (G2) |
| Medicine and biotech | Plants and chemicals, antibiotics, vaccines (G1), biologics (G2), gene therapies (G3) |
| Communications | Copper and glass, cables and fiber, switching hardware (G1), networks (G3), satellite constellations (G3) |
| Energy | Fuels, turbines, generating plants, grids (G3), storage systems (G3) |

### 1.5 Interlinks

- Resource chains tie to the grid (R10 to R12), since refining, smelting and fabs are the biggest energy users.
- Bottleneck materials tie to diplomacy: sanctions, export controls, embargoes, and monopolies.
- Compute capacity ties to research speed, finance, AI, surveillance and weapons.
- Pollution and waste follow the chain (W18), and recycling shortens it.

## 2. Characters, families and the narrative arc

### 2.1 Scale and detail

There are hundreds of families and thousands of tracked characters, so detail has to be tiered.

- **Notable characters.** Rulers, heirs, spouses, advisors, generals, governors, scholars, prophets, merchants. Full detail.
- **House members.** Lighter detail (age, role, alliances, traits).
- **Background families.** Aggregated statistics only. A background person is promoted to notable when events involve them, and demoted when they stop mattering.

### 2.2 What a character has

- Attributes and skills (administration, diplomacy, war, intrigue, learning, stewardship).
- Traits (born, learned, scarred), ambitions, beliefs, culture, religion.
- Health, age, education, career, wealth, land.
- Relationships (kin, spouse, friends, rivals, mentors, patrons) with opinion and history.
- Memories: grievances and favors that persist and can pass to heirs.

### 2.3 Life cycle

Birth, childhood and education, coming of age, marriage, children, careers, illness, old age, death. Fertility and mortality follow the demography model (P2), so wars, famines and epidemics reach noble houses too.

### 2.4 Marriage and alliances

Marriage forms alliances and merges claims. Customs vary by culture: dowry and bride price, consanguinity limits, concubinage, arranged versus chosen. Fertility, compatibility and religion affect outcomes. Marriage is the main link between houses, entities and corporate families.

### 2.5 Succession and inheritance

- Laws: primogeniture, ultimogeniture, partible inheritance, elective, tanistry, matrilineal, with legitimacy rules.
- Claims, regency for minors, succession crises, civil wars over inheritance. On a ruler's death the rules determine eligible candidates, lawful authority passes or a dispute starts, inheritance and administrative effects apply, regency resolves where needed, and the player's controlled leadership updates at the next permissible decision point. Institutions keep identities separate from officeholders, so bureaucracy, laws, debts and obligations survive a change of ruler or dynasty.
- Land and wealth fragment or concentrate depending on the law.

### 2.6 Houses and dynasties

A house has wealth, land, reputation, feuds, naming traditions, cadet branches and possible extinction. A dynasty is a house that holds a seat for generations and accumulates prestige. Merchant houses, clerical lineages and scientific schools follow the same logic.

### 2.7 Interpersonal stories

Friendship, courtship, affairs, scandal, rivalry, feud and vendetta, patronage, mentorship, betrayal, conspiracy, coup, forgiveness and reconciliation. These come from relationship and memory rules, not scripts.

### 2.8 Great people

Scientists, generals, prophets, artists, reformers and merchants emerge from conditions (education, wealth, need, culture, chance). They shift techs, battles, faiths and fashions, and they age and die.

### 2.9 Organizations of people

Courts, councils, guilds, clergies, officer corps, corporate boards, party leadership. Each has seats filled by characters with agendas.

### 2.10 Culture-specific kinship

Patrilineal, matrilineal and clan systems, naming conventions, honor and shame codes, hospitality law.

### 2.11 Story engine

- It watches the simulation for patterns (a family rising, a feud, a succession crisis, a famine, a discovery).
- It tags arcs and writes them into the chronicle.
- Stories span scales, from a single marriage to the fall of an empire.
- The same engine produces special-project stories (such as the FTL race).

### 2.12 Where the player sits

The player always plays as the character leading the selected country and can switch countries at any time, as with Europa Universalis tags. The ruler's traits, health, family and ambitions shape what the player can do, and play continues as the heir when the ruler dies. Characters around the ruler are actors with ambitions, who can be loyal, scheming or indifferent. Policies, appointments and marriages are the levers. Available decisions depend on the ruler's powers and the country's institutions, so control is neither universal command nor unrestricted manipulation, and an illegal action becomes an explicit political act with resistance and consequences. Switching happens at phase boundaries. A country the player leaves returns to AI control and keeps its resources, achievements and obligations, and the player does not inherit previous-country intelligence as an authorized asset.

## 3. Politics, espionage and sabotage

- **Agents and networks.** Spies are characters with skills and cover. Networks build up inside a target over time.
- **Intelligence.** Collect information on armies, tech, finance, plots. Quality depends on network depth. Counterintelligence finds and turns agents. Estimates carry ranges, confidence and observation dates, different agencies can hold contradictory assessments, and an estimate is never treated as objective truth. Attribution is separate from detection.
- **Operations.** Tech theft, sabotage of grids, factories and supply chains, subversion (funding factions, bribing officials), propaganda, cyber operations, assassination, kidnapping.
- **Attribution and deniability.** Operations can be traced, and exposure creates incidents, casus belli and diplomatic loss.
- **Court and corporate intrigue.** Plots, secrets, blackmail, schemes against rivals inside one's own entity.
- **Domestic politics.** Policy proposals, coalition building, lobbying, patronage and corruption networks, vote bargaining.
- **Coups and revolutions.** Prepared through networks and triggered by crises. Success depends on the military, the police and public mood.
- **Interlinks.** Espionage reaches K8, M12, E10, D11. Sabotage reaches R11, R22, P17.

## 4. Warfare, deepened

- **Command.** Characters lead armies and theatres. Their skill, loyalty and ambitions matter. Forces form a flexible hierarchy (personnel, formation, army or fleet, operational command, theatre, strategic command). Wars end through negotiation, surrender, ceasefire, collapse, withdrawal, political change, exhaustion or stalemate, with no universal warscore forcing peace.
- **Operational planning.** Campaigns with objectives, phases and reserves. Orders take time to arrive (N12), units can act on outdated orders while new ones are in transit, and modern communications cut delay but add electronic vulnerability.
- **Intelligence fog.** Armies see only what scouts, spies and satellites reveal.
- **Morale, veterans and doctrine.** Experience, culture and tradition shape performance.
- **Weather and season.** Mud, snow, monsoon, river floods, drought change movement and supply.
- **Army disease and attrition.** Camp disease, supply failures, heat and cold.
- **War economy.** Mobilization, rationing, war bonds, industrial conversion.
- **Occupation policy.** Garrison, assimilation, extraction, resistance, collaborators.
- **Scorched earth and siege.** Deliberate destruction, starvation, flooding by breaking dams.
- **Naval.** Blockades, convoys, sea control, ports, straits.
- **Atrocity and legitimacy.** Actions change reputation, unrest and postwar outcomes.
- **Postwar.** Reparations, partition, reconstruction, veterans and refugees.

## 5. Space, Earth-facing scope

Only space's effects on Earth are in scope. Habitats, bases, mining and interplanetary logistics are out. The FTL project is the single reach beyond Earth, as the endgame trigger.

- Launch capability and cost, orbital slots.
- Satellite services: communication, navigation, weather, Earth observation, surveillance. Societies and militaries depend on them and become vulnerable.
- Space debris and space law.
- Planetary defense: detecting and deflecting asteroids.
- Space-derived Earth economy: launch industry, satellite manufacturing, power beamed to grids.
- Space weather hitting grids and satellites (W25).

## 6. More mechanics and the relationship web

### 6.1 The relationship web

Every actor connects to every other through typed links, and the same link types appear at every scale:

- **Kinship and marriage.** Between characters, houses, entities.
- **Patronage and loyalty.** Between rulers, advisors, officers, clients.
- **Debt and obligation.** Between houses, banks, states, corporations.
- **Alliance and rivalry.** Between entities, factions, religions, corporations.
- **Trade and dependence.** Between nodes, firms, entities.
- **Faith and doctrine.** Between believers, clergy, sects, states.
- **Hidden links.** Secret agents, conspiracies, smuggling rings.

The cost is controlled by limiting the full web to notable actors and using aggregate links for the rest.

### 6.2 Additional mechanics

- **Memory and grievance.** Actors remember favors and wrongs. Grudges pass between generations and between entities.
- **Reputation.** Honor, trustworthiness, competence, piety, wealth. Reputation changes what others will offer.
- **Institution lifecycle.** Guilds, universities, monasteries, banks, courts and ministries rise, ossify, reform or decay.
- **Guilds and crafts.** Artisan knowledge, apprenticeships, craft secrets. Ties to lost knowledge (K10).
- **Mythology and origin stories.** Peoples hold founding stories that shape identity and legitimacy.
- **Historiography.** Chroniclers and historians shape how events are remembered, which feeds legitimacy and claims.
- **Toponymy.** Places are named by cultures and renamed after conquest or migration.
- **Festivals and rituals.** Calendar events that bind communities and boost cohesion.
- **Customary law and honor codes.** Vendetta, blood price, hospitality, oaths.
- **City identity.** Cities develop an industrial, mercantile, scholarly, religious or military character.
- **Folk heroes and outlaws.** Banditry can produce legendary figures who shift faction moods.
- **Wonders and monuments.** Special projects that lift prestige, heritage and cultural pull.
- **Corporate dynasties.** Founding families inherit and fight over firms.
- **Climate refugees and social displacement.** Large-scale movement stressing hosts.
- **Public health stories.** Plagues, reformers, quarantines, contested policies.

## 7. The Living Constitutional Order

Rules are in Final Master Vision 9.15 and Mechanics L1 to L10. This section shows how they play.

### 7.1 How a government exists

There is no government type. Each turn the game derives the political profile from the Governance Ledger: who holds which powers, how they are checked, who participates, how dissent is treated, who owns what. A kingdom can hold a powerful hereditary monarch, an independent judiciary, a liberal commercial economy, an elected provincial assembly and restrictive religious laws at once.

### 7.2 Worked example: House Veridane

House Veridane rules for 700 years. Its rulers create provincial councils, grant merchant privileges, consolidate the courts, restrict some religious practices, widen suffrage, crush several rebellions and strike successive settlements with the nobility. When the dynasty finally codifies, the draft is not a menu of generic options. It contains guild protections from 400 years earlier, provincial autonomy clauses from a civil war settlement, royal emergency powers inherited from an ancient invasion and religious compromises from a past sovereign. The constitution is a compressed, playable history of the civilisation.

### 7.3 The codification event chain

1. A trigger opens the moment (crisis, settlement, revolution, peace, judicial consolidation).
2. The draft assembles from ledger entries weighted by precedent.
3. Factions dispute articles on prerogatives, representation, courts, taxation, military command, religion and provinces.
4. The player negotiates articles, trades concessions, accepts a compromise, imposes a settlement or abandons the attempt.
5. Ratification by whatever the institutions require.
6. The result, and how it was reached, becomes precedent.

Each stage can use the illustrated event showcase already established in the concept screens.

### 7.4 How orders decay without changing their text

- A provision lets the sovereign dissolve the legislature. Each dissolution adds precedent weight, until representative government is hollow while the text is unchanged.
- An emergency decree is renewed and upheld by courts for generations until it is an entrenched convention.
- Elections stay but lose competitiveness, courts stay but lose independence, legislatures stay but lose real powers.

### 7.5 Validation cases

Germany 1933 (constitutional appointment, emergency decree, enabling act, rapid loss of competition and independence), Roman Republic to Principate, Magna Carta's reinterpretation over centuries, Meiji Constitution, post-colonial successor states inheriting and reinterpreting charters.

## 8. Autonomous institutions in play

Rules are in Final Master Vision 9.16 and Mechanics I1 to I10.

- **Arc of a chartered company.** A merchant association receives a charter with monopoly rights. It builds forts and ships to protect trade, raises a private army, collects taxes, signs treaties and governs millions without being a state. The home government then faces a choice: rely on it, regulate it, nationalise it, or let it fail. Each choice enters the Governance Ledger as precedent (the East India companies are calibration cases).
- **Arc of an industrial firm.** A private enterprise grows, is nationalised in a war, becomes a state monopoly, is privatised decades later, split by antitrust, and becomes a listed multinational.
- **Science as an actor.** A university or corporate lab can make a discovery the state did not fund, hold the patent, license it abroad and set an industry standard. In late eras a company may out-compute and outspend most governments.
- **Power dimensions.** A foreign company may own a mine (ownership), operate it under a host licence (authority), run the town around it (administration) and fund a local party (influence). Each dimension can be contested separately.
- **Ties to characters.** Founding families, boards and directors are characters, so corporate dynasties, boardroom feuds and succession fights use the same character systems as noble houses.

## 9. Warfare, deepened further

Extends section 4. Rules are in Final Master Vision 9.17 and Mechanics M29 to M44.

### 9.1 Who decides at each scale

| Scale | Typical decider | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strategic | Player as ruler, cabinet, legislature | War aims, mobilisation, budgets, alliances, escalation, peace terms |
| Operational | Player or commanders, by delegation mode | Offensives, defensive lines, reserves, supply corridors, sieges, landings |
| Tactical | Simulated commanders | Deployment, frontage, engagement, retreat, surrender |

### 9.2 Delegation in practice

| Mode | Player does | Cost | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct | Sets routes, objectives, formations, reserves | Command delay, institutional limits, attention | Orders arrive late or are misread |
| Guided | Sets objectives and constraints, approves plans | Approval time | Plans reflect commander judgement |
| Autonomous | Sets a broad mandate | Less information | Independent commander action |

### 9.3 A campaign from plan to peace

1. Strategic aims and authorisation (legislature, cabinet or ruler, per the constitutional order).
2. Mobilisation draws real people from cohorts and real equipment from inventories.
3. Campaign plan with objectives, boundaries, timing, formations, reserves, logistics and fallback, checked for feasibility on known information.
4. Operational events (movement, supply, orders) and engagements resolve at their own timestamps inside phases.
5. Engagement stages: contact, deployment, participation, attrition, morale and cohesion, then withdrawal, pursuit, surrender or continued fighting.
6. Conditional interruptions bring the player back only when something strategically significant happens.
7. War ends by negotiation shaped by objectives, endurance, alliances and domestic politics, not a warscore bar.
8. Occupation, resistance, veterans, refugees, debts, reforms and grievances continue as history.

### 9.4 Frontage and geography

A 200,000-strong army cannot bring its full strength through a narrow pass, while a small force behind a river crossing can hold. Approach corridors, frontage capacity, depth, chokepoints, crossing capacity, visibility, fortification coverage and retreat routes are derived from terrain, rivers, forests, wetlands, bridges, roads and settlements already in the map.

### 9.5 Logistics example

A corps whose rail link is cut keeps fighting on its depot stocks. Fuel runs out first, then ammunition, then food, each at its actual consumption rate. Its readiness falls stage by stage, and civilian traffic on the same line is disrupted, which raises prices behind the front and shifts public opinion.

### 9.6 War in politics

A victorious general gains a following that a ruler must manage. Arms suppliers grow rich while farmers lose labour to conscription. Workers radicalise in long wars. Legislatures, houses, corporations and clergy each take positions, and their reactions feed factions, approval and the Governance Ledger (emergency powers granted in war can become precedent).

### 9.7 Open design tension

Early-era phases can last years, so the player needs ways to steer an ongoing war without resolving hundreds of small events. Conditional military interruptions are the proposed answer, and their exact triggers still need specifying and testing.

## 11. The Living Scientific and Institutional Research Ecosystem

Rules are in Final Master Vision sections 9.6 and 9.16 and Mechanics K22 to K39 and I11 to I18. This section shows how institutional research develops in play.

### 11.1 Before formal research institutions

Early peoples gain knowledge through observation, experimentation, hunting, agriculture, construction, healing, navigation and craft. Knowledge is spread among individuals, families, occupational groups and oral traditions. No formal institution is needed for knowledge to emerge, but limited surplus, communication, record-keeping and specialist labour constrain the scale, continuity and complexity of organised research.

### 11.2 The generalist institution

A developing kingdom founds a Royal Maester's Office. It keeps records, advises the ruler, studies agricultural conditions, investigates disease, develops construction methods and observes the sky. Its scholars cover several accessible research categories with a shared budget, personnel, archives, equipment and mandate, and its effectiveness depends on actual competence and resources. Another civilisation might reach the same capability through a temple school, guild, monastery, scholarly council or academy.

### 11.3 The emergence of specialised institutions

As knowledge grows, the Office meets rising scientific and organisational complexity. Medicine needs specialised training, facilities and records. Engineering needs mathematics, practical experiment and dedicated equipment. Agriculture needs field observation and knowledge of local conditions. The ruler can reform the Office, create specialised departments, charter independent institutions or support existing autonomous scholarly bodies, each with financial, political and organisational consequences. A centralised academy coordinates well across disciplines, while independent institutions compete, experiment and pursue their own aims. No single structure is universally best.

### 11.4 Subtree unlocking and institutional requirements

A civilisation discovers the principles of electricity, which makes new pathways scientifically accessible. Advanced electrical engineering still needs expertise, experimental equipment, materials, infrastructure and organised research competence. The civilisation can add an electrical engineering department to an academy, reform a technical institute, found a laboratory or contract an outside organisation. The technology graph records scientific accessibility separately from institutional eligibility, so a technology can be theoretically accessible but practically blocked. Informal discovery, diffusion and independent breakthroughs remain possible under their existing rules, but they do not by themselves create sustained domestic research capability.

### 11.5 The advanced research ecosystem

A mature scientific civilisation holds universities, public laboratories, corporate research divisions, military research agencies, private foundations, professional associations and international consortia, each with its own objectives, people, funding, infrastructure, competence, governance, relationships and history. Universities may favour fundamental science, corporate labs commercial applications, military agencies strategic technology, and public institutions infrastructure, medicine, agriculture and the environment. These priorities are emergent, not fixed by institution type. One institution can span disciplines through internal units, and several can compete in one discipline.

### 11.6 Worked example: Veridan's scientific institutions

An early Veridane ruler founds a generalist Royal Maester's Office to preserve knowledge and advise the court. Successors expand its archives, recruit scholars and support experiment, and agricultural and engineering expertise grows inside it. As the realm grows, medical and engineering bodies emerge: some stay subordinate to the royal institution, others gain independent charters. Later discoveries open electrical engineering, advanced chemistry and industrial research, and universities, technical colleges and commercial laboratories follow. In advanced eras Veridan may hold independent organisations in computing, nuclear science, materials, biotechnology and other accessible fields. The original Office may survive as an advisory academy, become a university, fragment into successors or vanish, and its lineage stays traceable. This is one possible history, not a scripted chain.

### 11.7 Research funding and the limits of money

A government raises research funding sharply. Institutions with spare capacity expand their programmes. Those short of specialists, laboratories, energy or materials cannot turn all of it into immediate output, so the extra money funds construction, education, recruitment, equipment and reform instead. That expands future capacity rather than producing discoveries at once. The player has to tell apart spending, competence and realised progress.

### 11.8 Scientific competition and technological divergence

Two civilisations have similar populations and economies. One builds strong medical and agricultural institutions, the other engineering, energy and computing. Their ecosystems develop different expertise, infrastructure, traditions and industrial ties, so each can lead in some domains while depending on the other in others. Trade, licensing, migration, collaboration and espionage narrow the gaps, but institutional and industrial dependencies stop advantages vanishing overnight.

### 11.9 Scientific migration and institutional decline

A government restricts science or persecutes a scholarly community. Some researchers emigrate, others change occupations, and some programmes go dormant. Receiving institutions gain expertise, mentorship networks and knowledge, while the originating ones lose competence even if archives and buildings survive. Recovery needs political reform, funding, recruitment, education and rebuilt relationships. All of this runs through the existing migration, character, institutional, political and knowledge systems.

### 11.10 Institutional destruction and knowledge preservation

War destroys a major research centre. The consequences depend on what was lost: buildings, equipment, experimental records, archives, researchers, designs or organisational ties. Theory may survive elsewhere while specialised practical competence disappears, or distributed archives, surviving staff and successor institutions may preserve much of it. Reconstruction restores only what is actually recovered or rebuilt.

### 11.11 Interdisciplinary research and frontier science

Frontier work increasingly combines disciplines. A frontier computing programme may depend on physics, mathematics, materials science, electronics, precision manufacturing, energy infrastructure and specialised computing expertise. No single institution needs all of them: several can collaborate through shared facilities, contracts, joint programmes or consortia, with coordination, compatibility, intellectual property, funding, secrecy and governance shaping how well it works.

### 11.12 The FTL project

The FTL project is the culmination of institutional and interdisciplinary development. Sponsors must assemble the scientific, engineering, industrial, computational, financial and organisational capabilities under the existing special-project rules, with universities, corporations, state agencies, private laboratories and consortia contributing different parts. Money cannot substitute for missing fundamental knowledge, specialists, experimental infrastructure or physical prerequisites. Scientific contributions, financing, infrastructure, materials, intellectual property and governance control stay separately attributable. The existing FTL completion conditions, fictional assumptions and endgame rules are unchanged.

### 11.13 Historical validation cases

Ancient temple scholarship, royal scholarly patronage, the House of Wisdom, medieval universities, scientific academies, industrial research laboratories, Soviet scientific institutions, Bell Laboratories, the Manhattan Project, CERN, international scientific collaboration and contemporary semiconductor research networks. Validation checks the emergence, organisation, specialisation, financing, autonomy, coordination, competence, diffusion and resilience of research systems, not the reproduction of particular historical outcomes.

### 11.14 Interface representation

The research ecosystem uses the existing four levels. The Living Survey Map shows research institutions and infrastructure as contextual layers. WS-E1 Technology Tree shows accessibility and scientific and institutional prerequisites. WS-E2 Research, Knowledge Diffusion and Innovation (with showcase view WS-E2/V1 Scientific and Institutional Research Ecosystem) shows programmes, competence, allocation, discovery progress, diffusion and research networks. WS-C1 Government and Administration shows charters, governance, reform, autonomy and organisational relationships. WS-E3 Materials and Industrial Engineering and WS-E4 Energy, Infrastructure and Utility Networks show industrial, material, energy and infrastructure dependencies. Institution dossiers show personnel, leadership, budgets, departments, infrastructure, mandates, intellectual property, discoveries, collaborations and history. Analytical investigations cover bottlenecks, comparative performance, decline, diffusion, historical development and counterfactual reforms. All views read the same authoritative state, with no separate research simulation and no new interface level.

### 11.15 Calibration and open numerical parameters

Research categories, subtree boundaries, competence thresholds, maturity rates, specialisation costs, output units, discovery hazard coefficients, coordination costs and competence decay rates are set in the mechanics specification, dependency analysis and calibration passes. The architecture fixes relationships and constraints, not balancing numbers.

## 12. Living Knowledge Order and Living Historiography (added 24 Nov 2026)

Worked examples for Final Master Vision 9.18 and Mechanics section Q (K40 to K62, X16 to X27, A14, A15, U11). All names, places and outcomes below are illustrative. No numeric value here is a tuned parameter: wherever a quantity appears it is a symbol to be calibrated in the pass that owns it.

### 12.1 One event, four perspectives

A river city falls after a siege. The objective record (X10) holds one event: attacker, defender, dates, casualties by cohort, buildings destroyed, artifacts lost, ownership transfer. Four accounts are then written from that one event, each from what its author could observe (X16, X20):

- **The victor's court chronicler** had access to the commander's dispatches and the triumph. The account is accurate on the assault order, understates the attacker's losses and frames the fall as just punishment for a broken treaty (the treaty exists in the Governance Ledger; the breach is the chronicler's interpretation).
- **A defender's monastic annalist** fled with three refugee families. The account names the burned library correctly, overstates the massacre (built from refugee testimony, which is real but partial) and blames a gate-keeper's betrayal that never happened in the objective record. The rumour entered through testimony; it is a false claim with real provenance.
- **A neutral merchant's letter** reaches a trading partner two seasons later through the trade network (K46). It is precise on prices, grain shortages and the closing of the river toll, and silent on politics.
- **A historian three centuries later** works from surviving copies of the first two, an archaeological layer of burnt grain (X22) and the merchant letter found in a recovered archive. The account corrects the casualty figure using the burn layer, keeps the betrayal story because no evidence refutes it, and is itself reassessed later (12.4).

No account reads hidden simulation variables. Each claim carries its source chain (K54), so the reader (X26) can show which parts are first-hand, which rest on one testimony and which are corroborated.

### 12.2 Natural history before anyone writes it

A volcanic eruption in 6,200 BCE lays an ash layer, changes a river course and depresses harvests across a region for some years. Nobody records it. The objective record holds it from the moment it happens (X23). Oral tradition in two cultures carries a flood-and-fire story (X19), which survives as a distorted claim. Five thousand years later a geological society with the right methods (K62) dates the layer, links it to the river shift and publishes a finding. A later disagreement about the date is a real scholarly dispute resolved through replication (K56). The finding never changes the past; it changes what actors believe about it, and through X24 it can feed a cultural identity claim about the ancient flood.

### 12.3 Province and institutional history

The same river valley is held over its history by a chiefdom, two kingdoms, an empire, a colonial administration and a republic, and is renamed four times. Its history follows its permanent identity (X18): one continuous record, with each holder's chronicles attached as perspectives. A university founded under the empire survives the colonial period under a new charter (I1 succession), loses part of its collection in a fire, recovers copies through a sister institution (K52), and in the republic's era publishes a history of itself that leaves out its colonial-period role (X20 bias grounded in its incentives). An external historian later corrects that history using the charter in the Governance Ledger.

### 12.4 Reassessment

The betrayal story in 12.1 is challenged when a recovered garrison roll shows the named gate-keeper died a year before the siege. The claim is not deleted. A new edition (X21) revises it, both editions stay preserved and dated, and the reader shows the supersession chain. Popular memory may keep the betrayal story for generations after scholarly consensus drops it; consensus and public belief are separate quantities (K56, X24).

### 12.5 Comparative research across six civilisations

A scholarly institution sets out to compare the constitutional orders of six civilisations (K62). What it can achieve depends on access, not on counting books:

- Civilisation A: full archive access through a knowledge agreement (K59), its own language, high coverage.
- B: access through translations only; translations are copies of the same content and do not add independent evidence (K54).
- C: closed archives; only diplomatic reports and émigré testimony, both biased in known directions.
- D: dissolved two centuries earlier; evidence survives in a successor's archive and an archaeological record.
- E: an oral tradition culture; knowledge is held by living specialists and requires fieldwork and tacit transfer (K49).
- F: a rival that licenses access on condition that findings are reviewed before publication (rights and conditions in K43).

The resulting work's quality comes from coverage, source independence, source reliability, the team's competence and the method available to it (K53). Its conclusions about C and E carry wide uncertainty and the reader shows it. If the comparative work becomes widely cited, it may later be classified as a Grand Intellectual Work (K57), by the same criteria that would apply to any other work.

### 12.6 Acquisition mechanisms

One foreign metallurgy treatise can arrive by many routes, each through an existing system (K45): purchase in a market; a copy made by a travelling scholar; a gift in a diplomatic exchange; a licence; seizure in war (the copy moves, provenance records the seizure); theft by an intelligence network; a defecting craftsman who brings tacit knowledge but no text; a captured workshop that brings tools and partial knowledge; a translation commissioned from a third party; or an open publication anyone connected can read. Each method sets cost, legality, secrecy, provenance, delay and risk. A seized copy can later be the subject of a repatriation claim (K59). None of these routes grants capability on arrival: the treatise must still be absorbed (K48) and the capability must still be built (K50).

### 12.7 Six disciplines on one architecture

All six use the same artifact, claim, evidence and review machinery (K40, K41, K53 to K56). Findings change what actors know and can decide, never what exists (K62).

- **Constitutional scholarship.** Reads the Governance Ledger as evidence; a treatise arguing that a decree was ultra vires can feed a legitimacy challenge through L-series courts, but only the court decides validity.
- **Sociology.** Surveys and fieldwork on accessible populations; access is limited by language, censorship and trust; findings inform policy proposals, not population state.
- **Demography.** Censuses are observations of the population model with coverage error; a census-poor state has worse estimates of its own population and plans worse.
- **Geology.** Surveys raise an actor's knowledge of where resources are; they never create the deposit (R-series owns it).
- **Environmental science.** Detecting a pollution trend or climate change requires instruments and time series; the trend exists in the objective state whether or not anyone measures it.
- **Economics and finance.** Models of the actor's own economy built from its statistics; a bad model leads to bad policy through ordinary decision systems.

### 12.8 Digital era: one open paper, six civilisations

A laboratory in civilisation A publishes an open paper on a new battery chemistry (K47). The content is one artifact; reach is resolved through publication scopes and lazy queries, not a copy per reader (A15).

- **A** already has the materials, the industry and trained engineers: it moves through prototype to commercial feasibility on its own production timescale (K50).
- **B** is connected and has strong universities but lacks the refining capacity: knows and understands, cannot deploy until the production chain exists.
- **C** censors the domain: the paper is reachable only through restricted channels; diffusion is slower but never zero (the Sentinelese principle).
- **D** has the industry but weak absorption in this discipline: needs time, hires, or joint research to understand it (K48).
- **E** is under export restrictions on the precursor materials (K59): understands it fully and cannot build it.
- **F** independently reached the same result a year earlier but published nothing: it has the capability with no first-mover prestige (K58), and the duplication is real, not double-counted.

Open publication reduces A's first-mover rent and raises global diffusion: a feedback loop listed in Mechanics section Q.

### 12.9 Capability development and failure

A civilisation that knows how to build a steam engine (K44 state understood) still passes through demonstration, prototype, limited reproduction and commercial feasibility (K50). Each step needs specific inputs: metallurgy grade, machining tolerance, fuel supply, trained workers, capital and demand. A prototype can fail; the failure produces knowledge (what went wrong) and a cost. A state can stall at limited reproduction for generations if fuel or demand is missing. Knowledge without the dependency stack is not capability.

### 12.10 Regression and restoration

An empire's aqueduct engineering depends on a guild, quarries, lime production and state funding. Civil war breaks funding; the guild disperses; within two generations the aqueducts are repaired badly and then not at all. The treatises survive in monasteries (documentation survives; K51). The capability state moves to "known but no longer reproducible". Restoration later needs more than reading: it needs masons trained through practice (tacit, K49), lime kilns and an authority able to fund multi-year works. A successor state that recovers the texts and invites foreign masons restores faster than one that has only the texts.

### 12.11 Causal structure and candidate mathematics

These are candidate formulations for engineering evaluation (Computational Doctrine section 19). None is selected; no parameter is set.

- **Absorption.** Absorbed understanding as a stock that rises toward the accessible frontier at a rate set by discipline competence and falls with turnover and disuse: a first-order relaxation, solved exactly per legal Δt through the propagator cache.
- **Capability transitions.** Each K50 step as a hazard whose rate depends on satisfied dependencies (a product of saturating terms, zero when a hard prerequisite is missing); sampled with time-to-event draws from the counter-based RNG.
- **Regression.** The same machinery in reverse: a hazard for dropping a state when dependencies decay below thresholds.
- **Synthesis quality.** A bounded function of coverage, effective independent evidence (after collapsing copies and circular citations through the dependency graph), reliability and competence; saturating, never linear in source count.
- **Diffusion of open content.** Reach computed per scope as eligibility (connection, permission, language, capability) times an uptake hazard; evaluated lazily when an actor queries, not by pushing to every actor.
- **Consensus.** A belief state per community moved by replication and review events, with separate public, political and religious acceptance quantities; candidate forms include bounded opinion dynamics.
- **Historical memory.** Per-group belief over claims, updated by accessible accounts, education and propaganda, decaying without transmission; feeds legitimacy and grievance only through X24.

Causal chain, in words: events produce objective records; observation produces artifacts; artifacts move through acquisition and transmission; absorption turns access into understanding; research and synthesis produce new artifacts; capability needs understanding plus a real dependency stack; deployment changes the economy and military; those changes produce new events.
